Dachshund70 – Testing AI on Real Hardware Development

A wireless keyboard I'm designing from scratch to find out how much of a hardware product an AI can build: PCBs, a switch plate, a machined case, firmware, a configuration protocol, a driver and a desktop app. Claude Code does most of the drafting, routing, coding and CAD. I set the direction, make the calls and review every result, and I keep a record of where my judgement changed the design.

// STATUS: PRE-PCB ORDER

The boards are designed, the firmware is built and tested on a PC and in CI, and the case is on its third revision. No PCBs have been ordered yet, so nothing has run on real hardware. The schedule is in section 06.

Design → Engineering: every one of the AI's designs passed its own checks. The problems I caught were about how a part is pressed, printed, assembled or machined, which is where my industrial design training does the work.

01 // 09

The experiment

AI tools are starting to do work that engineers have always done. I wanted first-hand evidence of how far that goes in hardware, where a mistake costs a board order or a machined part. So I picked a product big enough to test it properly: a tri-mode keyboard with its own electronics, firmware, software and enclosure.

I set up the project so the results would be honest. Claude Code does the legwork, and I make every decision that has more than one reasonable answer. Each change goes through a pull request with CI, the repository logs which calls were mine, and I review the CAD and PCBs myself in Fusion 360 and KiCad before anything is kept. Where I can, I check the design physically with printed test parts.

The project was called the Dachshund60F until November 2026, when I renamed it the Dachshund70.

// THE BOARD

  • Layout: 60% with an F-row, hot-swap switches and screw-in stabilisers
  • Input: a Bourns PEC12R encoder on its own module, used as a roller that turns like a mouse wheel
  • Mount: FR4 plate on six gasket points with silicone socks, between a top and bottom shell
  • Power: 1S LiPo, MCP73831 charger with a load-sharing power path
  • Status: five indicator LEDs and a status screen (touch e-paper is the current direction)
  • Boards: mainboard, USB-C daughterboard, encoder module, screen board, dongle and plate, all in KiCad 10

What the AI Did

  • Firmware & Tests Zephyr firmware over 15 phases, a hardware-free keymap engine with host unit tests, and a fuzzed protocol parser.

What I Did

  • Scope & Architecture Chose the MCU, the wired-first build order, custom firmware over a QMK or ZMK fork, and a driverless HID design.
  • Engineering Trade-offs Picked between workable options for the power path, charge current, LED logic levels, polling rate, encoder and screen.
  • Design Review Reviewed every board and case revision as a product someone will press, assemble and machine. Most of the AI's serious mistakes turned up here (section 04).
  • Industrial Design Set the case form, materials, gasket mount, accent ring, the roller's look and feel, and which top shell goes to production.
03 // 09

Decisions I made

A selection from the decision log in the repository. Each one had more than one reasonable answer.

Decision What I Chose Why
Microcontroller nRF52840 on the keyboard and the dongle RP2040 and STM32 have no Bluetooth. Using one chip family at both ends means one toolchain.
Build Order Tri-mode, built wired first Wireless doubles the firmware. Getting USB solid first keeps radio bugs separate from keymap bugs.
Driver A vendor HID interface plus a user-space library, with no kernel driver Kernel drivers need signing on Windows and entitlements on macOS. This works on any computer with nothing to install.
Power Path Load-sharing circuit (P-MOSFET + Schottky) on the charger The first design only ran from the battery. Now it runs from USB with the switch off or no cell fitted.
LED Data Line SN74LV1T34 level-shifting buffer On USB the LEDs see about 4.7 V, so a 3.3 V data line has no margin. I chose a proper buffer over a diode drop.
Polling Rate Stay at 1 kHz on the nRF52840 and cut latency in firmware Going higher meant a new module, bootloader and board. Debounce and tap-hold are the bigger delays, and those I control.
Screen Placement The screen and status LEDs moved off the mainboard onto their own board The mainboard flexes with the gasket-mounted plate, and a rigid screen shouldn't. It also lets the same mainboard go into a cheaper version with no screen or encoder.
Screen Type Touch e-paper, after trying a Sharp memory LCD and an AMOLED I'll mostly use the screen to change settings, and an AMOLED showing static settings screens risks burn-in.
Case Weight Dropped the brass weight and lowered the whole stack onto a foam floor It lets the plate, PCB and keycaps sit 2 mm lower with a thinner battery. I can design a weight later.
Fasteners Heat-set inserts and flush countersunk screws Inserts survive the case being opened again and again, so I reverted a change to thread-forming screws. Flush heads keep the bottom clean.
04 // 09

Issues I caught

The AI's work passed its own checks: DRC was clean and the tests were green. The serious problems were physical ones, about how a part moves under a finger, how a gasket seats or how a case gets made. I found them by reviewing the design as an object someone will use, which is what my industrial design training taught me to do.

Catch 01: Wrong Encoder Orientation

The roller needs an encoder whose shaft runs parallel to the board, so it turns like a mouse wheel. The AI put the PEC12R-4220F in the BOM: Bourns' axial part, whose shaft points straight up like a volume knob. Its own cross-check had the two part families backwards, and the footprint was right, so nothing automated flagged it. I caught the mismatch and moved it to the radial PEC12R-22xxF family.

Catch 02: A Button You Couldn't Press

The AI treated the encoder's click as a press down on the wheel. On this part the switch is pressed along the shaft (0.5 mm of travel), and the end of the wheel was buried inside the case wall. The model looked fine, and nobody could have used the button. I had the wheel brought out through the end wall so a finger can push its end in.

Catch 03: Gaskets Facing the Wrong Way

The AI misread my description of the plate's gasket tabs, then modelled the gaskets pointing out toward the case wall, where they couldn't seat under the bezel. I had the tabs redrawn as branched nubs with the silicone socks lying along the plate edge. In rev 3 the retainers screw up into the top shell, so the shell, plate, socks and retainers come out as one sub-assembly.

Catch 04: A Case You Couldn't Machine

After many rounds of edits, the AI's top shell was full of leftovers: stray holes, thin slivers, patched faces and cylinder stubs that read as holes from outside. None of it failed a check, and all of it would cost money on a CNC quote. I had shell A rebuilt as one clean outer skin with a single underside pocket and consistent corner radii.

More Catches

  • Screw Heads Standing Proud I printed a test case, and the bottom screw heads stood out of the surface. The AI had cut the countersinks at about Ø5.8 mm, too small for a DIN 7991 M3 head. They're now Ø6.6 mm at 90°, with the heads 0.3 mm below the face to leave room for anodising.
  • Bare Patches in the Ground Pour I spotted gaps in the back copper pour around several switches. They were pockets fully boxed in by tracks, which KiCad strips as unconnected islands. Each one big enough now has a ground via.
  • A Header in the Way of the Battery The SWD debug header sat in front of the battery connector, so the plug couldn't go in. It moved to the back of the board.
  • Connectors on the Wrong Side Two connectors were on the front of the mainboard, which ruled out single-sided assembly at JLCPCB. Every part is now on the back, and the scattered ground vias went from 199 to 91.
  • An Open, Unfinished First Case The AI's first case left the screen uncovered, showed the plate beside the arrow keys, and had a 510 g brass weight exposed through the bottom with its screws facing the wrong way.
05 // 09

How the design changed

The AI's first case was a parametric CadQuery script at a 7° typing angle. I moved the case into Fusion 360, where I can review and edit the model directly, and directed three revisions from there. Rev 3 came from a 14-point list I wrote after reviewing rev 2.

Two Top Shells, One Choice

I asked for a second top shell to compare against the sloped one: shell B, with flat sides and a front thumb slope. Both use the same bottom shell, plate and boards. I chose shell A for production. It also needs less aluminium (131 cm³ against 166 cm³), which matters on a CNC quote.

  • Plate, PCB and keycaps 2 mm lower, on a 1.2 mm floor with room for 3 mm of foam
  • A thinner 7 mm battery, turned and moved to the back
  • Eight hidden M3 countersunk screws into heat-set inserts
  • A split PETG version of the case for test prints on a 256 mm printer bed

Status & schedule

The electronics and firmware are done on paper and on a PC, and I'm finishing the case and the screen now. I'm also designing a wired version alongside it, with an RP2350 and a USB hub on the back. The next steps, in order:

01
Order PCBs: the mainboard, USB-C daughterboard, encoder module, screen board, dongle and FR4 plate, with assembly at JLCPCB. The roller encoders have a 16-week lead time, so they get ordered first.
02
Bring-up & testing: flash the bootloader and firmware, then work through the bring-up plan: rail voltages, current draw in every power state, scan rate, latency and radio range. Fix what the real boards show.
03
Order the case: test-fit a printed PETG case around the real boards, then order the CNC aluminium shells and the laser-cut accent ring.
04
Final assembly: fit the switches, stabilisers, gaskets and battery, then test the finished keyboard over USB, Bluetooth and the dongle with Doghouse.
07 // 09

The software side

Firmware, Protocol & Driver

The firmware is written from scratch on Zephyr / nRF Connect SDK. The keymap logic is plain C with no hardware dependencies, so it runs as unit tests on a PC. One YAML file defines the configuration protocol, and the C and TypeScript code for both ends is generated from it.

  • USB: NKRO with boot-protocol fallback, media keys, suspend and remote wakeup.
  • Advanced keys: tap-hold, combos, tap dance, macros, Caps Word, Auto Shift, Leader, mouse keys.
  • Wireless: BLE HID with multiple hosts, and a 1 kHz 2.4 GHz link through the dongle.
  • Safety: every index and length from the host is checked, and the parser is fuzzed. A configuration channel that can write macros could otherwise make the keyboard type commands.
  • Process: one PR per phase, CI on every change, and versioned, CRC-checked settings in flash.

Doghouse Configurator

Doghouse edits keymaps, advanced keys, macros and settings, and installs firmware updates. It runs in Chrome or Edge over WebHID, or as a desktop app for Windows, macOS and Linux that updates itself. The boards are named after dogs, which is where the name comes from.

08 // 09

What I've learnt about AI in hardware

  • Where It Helps Routing passes, boilerplate, tests, documentation and CAD iterations come quickly and in large amounts.
  • Where I'm Needed Decisions with several workable answers, and anything about how a part moves, fits, feels or gets made. Every catch in section 04 passed the AI's own checks first.
  • CAD Drifts After many small edits, the AI's models collect leftover geometry it doesn't see. I now ask for clean rebuilds at each milestone and check the model myself in Fusion 360.
  • How I Keep It Honest Checks I can trust: DRC and ERC, tests that compare the pin map against the PCB, CI on every change, printed test parts, and a bring-up plan with measured numbers.

A keyboard looks simple, but doing one properly covers USB device design, Bluetooth LE, a proprietary radio link, SPI and I2C displays, addressable LEDs, flash storage and power management. I needed to understand each well enough to judge whether the AI's work was right. I'm taking Computer Architecture II (CEG3136) at uOttawa while building this and using it directly: flash memory maps, timers and interrupts for matrix scanning, and SPI and I2C display drivers.

USB

Descriptors, interfaces and report IDs; boot protocol vs NKRO for BIOS and KVM support; the 1 kHz polling ceiling of full-speed USB; USB-C sink design with CC pull-downs, ESD clamping and a PTC fuse.

Radio & Wireless

Antenna keep-outs on a module design, Bluetooth LE HID with bonding and multiple hosts, and Nordic's ESB protocol for a dongle link with a latency budget of about 1 ms.

Power

Load sharing between the charger and the system, keeping charge current inside a 500 mA USB budget, logic-level margins for SK6812 LEDs, and LiPo charging and safety.

PCB Design

KiCad 10 end to end with custom design rules, matched USB differential pairs, ground pours, single-sided assembly, and where an autorouter falls short.

Mechanical

Gasket mounts, heat-set inserts, countersink sizing, tolerances for printed and machined parts, and designing a shell for 3-axis CNC.

Firmware & Protocols

Zephyr and devicetree, debounce algorithms, wear-levelled settings storage, protocol versioning and code generation, and fuzzing a parser.
